# Mystagogia — Editorial Writing Standard

Approved by the owner on October 29, 2026. This is the standing editorial standard for Library and Grimoire writing, including research, outlines, chat drafts, MDX articles, substantive revisions, and copy edits. Every AI agent working on editorial content must read this document before writing or editing articles. Do not wait until an article is ready to enter the repository to apply it.

This document develops the content principles in [SPECIFICATION.md](SPECIFICATION.md), especially sections 2, 4, and 21. Use [EDITORIAL_WORKFLOW.md](EDITORIAL_WORKFLOW.md) for the complete production process, alongside the existing collection, citation, media, and publication documentation. Where older articles or previews exhibit weaker prose, this standard governs new work; their existence is not permission to repeat those weaknesses. An explicit later instruction from the owner takes precedence. This standard does not authorize new articles, changes to existing articles, or publication by itself.

## Library and Grimoire

Library articles must rest on credible academic research, primary historical sources, and verifiable evidence. Their purpose is to explain what the evidence supports about a subject and why it matters. Never invent historical details, citations, quotations, religious practices, or scholarly interpretations. An engaging narrative does not justify filling gaps in the record.

Unverified personal gnosis and original magical experimentation belong in the Grimoire, not the Library. Grimoire articles may present the author's experiences, methods, adaptations, and original ideas, clearly identifying their provenance and separating observations from interpretations. Never invent experiences on the author's behalf or present an original practice as ancient. Historical claims in Grimoire articles require the same care and attribution as historical claims in the Library.

The Library may explain documented modern traditions or practitioner claims when relevant, with clear attribution and historical context. Reporting that a person or community holds a belief does not establish that belief as historical fact or verify its supernatural claims. Do not turn such reporting into personal revelation, an original ritual, or unsupported advice to practitioners.

## Research and evidence

Read the relevant passages and supporting scholarship before building the narrative around them. Favor scholarly editions and translations, peer-reviewed research, academic books, inscriptions, archaeological publications, and authoritative museum or collection records. A search snippet, AI-generated research summary, popular retelling, or unsourced correspondence list is a lead to investigate, not adequate verification. Treat supplied research notes as material to evaluate rather than a guarantee that every claim and citation is correct.

Preserve distinctions between kinds of evidence. A myth records a story told about a deity; a scene in a comedy or tragedy is not automatically a record of ordinary worship. A ritual handbook documents instructions, not proof that every instruction was performed or effective. An inscription may document an offering or religious office without revealing an entire ceremony. A museum interpretation of an image should not be expanded into a universal ancient doctrine.

Give evidence its place, period, and cultural setting when these affect its meaning. Do not combine regional traditions or conflicting versions into a single invented narrative. Later Greek, Roman, late antique, and modern interpretations must not silently become evidence for earlier practice. Explain relevant uncertainty in proportion to its importance, and avoid claiming a universal custom from a single local example.

Distinguish what a source explicitly states from a scholar's interpretation and from the article's own reading. Identify a scholar and the supporting work when attributing an interpretation; do not use vague appeals to what scholars supposedly believe. Explain disagreements when they change how readers should understand the subject, without turning the article into a catalogue of every scholarly position. If a claim cannot be verified, omit it or identify the unresolved issue in editorial notes before review. Missing evidence is not permission to manufacture a reconstruction.

## Voice and paragraph development

Write engaging, accessible prose for an intelligent reader who may be unfamiliar with the subject. The result should be a readable, compelling article rather than a dry academic paper or a generic AI-generated summary. Use clear language, concrete details, and sufficient context to explain names, religious terms, historical settings, and unfamiliar concepts. Research should inform the narrative without overwhelming it. Avoid excessive academic jargon, unexplained terminology, lengthy literature reviews, and qualifications that obscure the point of a sentence.

Use substantial, naturally developed paragraphs. Each paragraph should carry an idea through its relevant evidence, explanation, or consequence, and connect coherently with the next. Vary sentence length and paragraph length according to the material. Do not impose a mechanical sentence count or pad a paragraph to make it look substantial. Equally, do not break a connected discussion into artificial one-line paragraphs for emphasis. A succession of short statements is not a substitute for development.

Avoid dramatic sentence fragments, manufactured suspense, inflated claims, and manufactured mystical grandeur. Let an attested event, myth, artifact, or religious practice supply the interest. Do not add invented dialogue, scenery, emotions, motives, or sensory details to make a historical account more vivid. Literary interpretation can be compelling when its basis is clear; it must not masquerade as additional ancient evidence.

Avoid formulaic introductions, predictable AI transitions, repeated contrast formulas, and repeated declarations that a detail is important, striking, or revealing. Explain the significance through the discussion itself. Phrases such as “throughout history,” “delve into,” “a rich tapestry,” “it is important to note,” and “in conclusion” should not serve as stock scaffolding. Do not replace them with another repeated set of verbal habits.

End when the article's argument or narrative has reached a useful conclusion. A closing passage may draw together a meaningful insight or return to an opening example, but it should not repeat every section, restate the same point in several paragraphs, or manufacture a grand final pronouncement. Do not append a predictable summary merely because other articles have one.

## Deity articles

Help readers understand the deity as a distinctive religious and mythological figure. Explore character, mythology, distinctive powers, historical worship, and religious significance through compelling narrative prose, giving each the depth warranted by the surviving evidence. Explain how the deity acts in particular stories, how those actions illuminate character and powers, and how historical communities understood and honored that deity.

Choose an opening suited to the subject: a well-attested story, a documented sanctuary or ceremony, an artifact, or a concrete historical question may provide a strong beginning. Establish enough context for readers to follow it. Do not force every article into a dramatic crisis, a generic definition of the deity, an origins-first biography, or the same sequence of sections.

Mythological narrative should do more than summarize a plot. Develop the relevant episode and explain what it contributes to an understanding of the deity, keeping its source and version clear. Worship deserves comparable attention where evidence permits: sanctuaries, festivals, offerings, priesthoods, civic roles, and household practices should appear in their historical circumstances. Do not invent a ritual to compensate for limited evidence of worship.

Discuss symbols, epithets, relationships, and iconography through their attested contexts and meanings. Readers should understand a deity's particular qualities and religious importance rather than merely memorize correspondences. Do not substitute lists of colors, crystals, incense, plants, planetary associations, or supposed preferences for an account of the deity. Any relevant association must have an identifiable source and an accurately described period and tradition.

Allow the evidence to determine emphasis. A deity with little surviving narrative mythology may be best understood through poetry, inscriptions, cult, or imagery. Do not manufacture a consistent personality from incompatible sources, make every deity an interchangeable patron of prosperity or protection, or impose modern moral judgments as though they were an ancient consensus.

## Organization and presentation

Organize the article around the subject's strongest evidence and the questions readers need answered. Use descriptive headings that help navigation without interrupting every developing thought. Section order and length should serve the particular article; neither a fixed template nor an arbitrary word count should create filler.

Narrative prose is the default. Avoid unnecessary bullet lists, correspondence tables, repeated key-takeaway boxes, and bold standalone slogans. Lists are appropriate when the material is genuinely sequential or easier to compare, especially procedures in the Grimoire, but they should not replace connected explanation in a Library article. Images and captions should contribute to understanding rather than serve as substitutes for the prose.

Research depth belongs in precise evidence and thoughtful explanation, not in an accumulation of names and references. Introduce scholarly discussion where it helps readers understand an actual problem. Keep bibliographic detail in citations and notes unless an author's identity or a work's historical setting matters to the narrative.

## Citations and historical images

Provide precise source citations close enough to the supported claims that readers can identify what is being substantiated. Cite quotations, substantial historical assertions, disputed claims, and attributed interpretations. Where a paragraph draws on different sources, place citations so their scope is clear. A general bibliography alone does not support every claim in an article.

For ancient works, provide verified book, section, passage, or line locators as appropriate. For modern scholarship, give verified pages or sections when available, with the correct edition and translation. Quote only wording checked against the identified source, preserving its meaning and context. Never invent page numbers, quotations, bibliographic metadata, DOI values, URLs, or a scholarly consensus. Do not imply that an inaccessible work has been read; use an accessible, adequate source or flag the limitation for editorial review.

In MDX, follow [ARTICLE_REFERENCES.md](ARTICLE_REFERENCES.md) and the existing citation implementation. Store reusable verified bibliographic records in `src/data/sources.ts`, with article-specific locators in citation metadata. Check that inline markers, note numbers, and source records agree. Existing articles demonstrate the system but are not authority for copying a numbering error or unsupported claim. Automated validation establishes structural integrity, not historical truth.

Historical images require accurate identification, dating where supported, provenance, captions, and rights attribution under [MEDIA_REGISTRY.md](MEDIA_REGISTRY.md) and [MDX_FIGURES.md](MDX_FIGURES.md). Distinguish an original artifact from a later copy, cast, reconstruction, or modern illustration. Generated artwork is a modern interpretation and cannot serve as evidence for ancient appearance or practice. Do not infer permission to use a photograph from the age of the object depicted.

## Editorial review and publication

Before handing over a draft, review its factual support and prose together. Confirm that claims match the cited passages, quotations and locators are accurate, historical settings remain distinct, and any interpretation is recognizable as such. Then read for paragraph development, flow, unnecessary repetition, formulaic phrasing, unsupported drama, and whether the reader will understand the subject as a distinctive figure. Remove filler rather than disguising it through paraphrase.

Follow the existing [editorial collection](EDITORIAL_COLLECTIONS.md), [registry](EDITORIAL_REGISTRIES.md), and [development workflow](DEVELOPMENT_WORKFLOW.md) requirements when preparing repository changes. Technical checks do not replace editorial judgment or owner review. Preserve the author's public anonymity and the distinction between Library research and Grimoire practice throughout text, metadata, and media.

Drafting, editing, source verification, image preparation, and publication are separate permissions. Keep the work within the owner's current instruction. Never change publication status, commit, push, or deploy merely because a draft or its checks are complete; explicit authorization is required for those actions.
