In the ancient Greek city of Elis, there stood a temple that opened only once each year. Even on that occasion, the public was forbidden to enter. The priest alone was permitted to pass into the sacred enclosure, while everyone else remained outside. When the traveler Pausanias visited Elis during the second century CE, he found this arrangement remarkable enough to record in his *Description of Greece*. The temple belonged to Hades, ruler of the underworld, and Pausanias suggested that the restriction reflected the nature of the god's kingdom. Just as the sanctuary admitted visitors on only one occasion each year, human beings entered the realm of Hades only once, with little expectation of ever returning. <sup id="citation-ref-1"><a href="#citation-note-1" aria-label="Read source note 1">[1]</a></sup>

The explanation was Pausanias's own interpretation, and the original reasons for the custom may have been more complicated. Nevertheless, it captures the solemnity surrounding a god whose authority extended over the final destination of mortal life. Zeus commanded the heavens, Poseidon governed the sea, and the other immortals frequently intervened in human affairs. Hades generally remained within his kingdom, receiving the dead who passed beyond the reach of their families and communities. His appearances in Greek mythology were comparatively infrequent, but no mortal could escape the eventual reach of his power.

Yet the god honored behind the closed doors at Elis possessed another identity, one that is often overlooked in modern accounts. The Greeks also knew him as Plouton, a name associated with the riches emerging from beneath the earth. Under this name, the ruler of the dead could be represented with a horn of plenty and approached through religious traditions concerned with fertility and abundance. His kingdom received the bodies of the dead, but the earth above it also produced the crops and resources that sustained the living. These seemingly contradictory associations belonged to a religious understanding of the world in which death and fertility were closely connected.

## The Brother of Zeus and Poseidon

Hades belonged to the generation of gods who established their rule after the overthrow of Kronos and the Titans. He was a son of Kronos and Rhea, and therefore a brother of Zeus, Poseidon, Hera, Demeter, and Hestia. According to Hesiod's account of the divine generations, Kronos swallowed his children because he feared that one of them would eventually overthrow him. Zeus escaped this fate and later compelled his father to release the other gods, beginning the struggle that secured the supremacy of the new divine order.

After the victory over the Titans, the three brothers divided the principal regions of the cosmos. In the fifteenth book of Homer's *Iliad*, Poseidon recalls that Zeus received the sky, he himself obtained the sea, and Hades was allotted the darkness beneath the earth. The land and Mount Olympus remained common to them. The division established Hades as a sovereign whose authority originated in the same settlement that gave his brothers their respective dominions. His position was neither a punishment imposed by Zeus nor an exile from the company of the gods. He possessed a kingdom of his own, and the other immortals generally respected its boundaries. <sup id="citation-ref-2"><a href="#citation-note-2" aria-label="Read source note 2">[2]</a></sup> <sup id="citation-ref-3"><a href="#citation-note-3" aria-label="Read source note 3">[3]</a></sup>

The nature of that kingdom explains much about the god's reputation. In Greek epic, the underworld was a vast and shadowy region inhabited by the souls of the deceased. Its geography included rivers associated with the boundaries of death, while its entrances and passages were guarded by supernatural beings. Hesiod describes the dwelling of Hades and Persephone beneath the earth, with Cerberus stationed at its entrance. The monstrous hound welcomed those arriving in the underworld but prevented its inhabitants from escaping, attacking anyone who attempted to cross the threshold in the opposite direction. <sup id="citation-ref-4"><a href="#citation-note-4" aria-label="Read source note 4">[4]</a></sup> <sup id="citation-ref-5"><a href="#citation-note-5" aria-label="Read source note 5">[5]</a></sup>

Hades himself was distinct from Thanatos, the divine personification of death. The Greeks recognized several figures associated with the dying and the dead, each with particular responsibilities. Hermes could guide departed souls toward the underworld, while the Keres represented destructive forms of death and other beings appeared in traditions concerning burial, judgment, or punishment. Hades governed the realm into which the deceased passed. He did not ordinarily travel through the mortal world causing people to die, and the surviving mythology rarely presents him as an active agent in the circumstances of individual deaths.

This distinction also helps explain why the Greek underworld should not be confused with the Christian conception of Hell. In the *Odyssey*, the dead include ordinary people, celebrated heroes, grieving parents, and figures subjected to extraordinary punishments. Their presence in Hades' kingdom does not necessarily indicate wrongdoing during life. The bleak existence described in early epic reflects a widespread concern with the loss of mortal vitality, while later religious traditions developed more elaborate expectations of judgment, blessedness, and punishment. Hades remained the sovereign of the realm containing these different conditions, although other divine or heroic figures could be assigned the task of judging the deceased.

## The God Who Could Not Be Persuaded

The most formidable quality attributed to Hades was his resistance to persuasion. In the ninth book of the *Iliad*, Phoenix attempts to persuade Achilles to abandon his anger toward Agamemnon and return to the fighting. During his argument, the elderly warrior explains that even gods can be influenced by prayers, offerings, and appeals for mercy. Hades serves as an exceptional comparison because he is regarded as especially unyielding. His refusal to be moved makes him hateful to mortals, whose pleas cannot ordinarily recover those who have entered his kingdom. <sup id="citation-ref-6"><a href="#citation-note-6" aria-label="Read source note 6">[6]</a></sup>

The observation reveals something important about how the Greeks understood divine power. Zeus could change his intentions, Apollo might accept compensation after inflicting punishment, and Poseidon could be approached through sacrifice when his anger threatened sailors. Hades represented a more permanent boundary. Families could mourn their dead, perform the proper funeral ceremonies, and continue honoring departed relatives, but the ordinary expectation was that those who entered the underworld would remain there. His severity was therefore closely connected to the finality of death rather than to an unusually cruel temperament.

This quality also helps account for his comparative absence from the countless disputes and adventures involving the Olympian gods. Hades was rarely portrayed wandering through Greece, competing for the attention of mortals, or intervening in the affairs of particular cities. His kingdom remained largely separate from the world above, and the myths in which he took an active role frequently involved some threat to that separation. Heroes might attempt to enter his domain, rescue someone who had died, or carry away a creature that belonged beneath the earth. Such stories were memorable precisely because the events they described were exceptional.

Even so, the literary tradition did not make Hades entirely incapable of negotiation. The myths surrounding Persephone, Herakles, and Orpheus present circumstances in which the ruler of the dead could respond to the demands of other gods or extraordinary mortals. His authority was immense, but it operated within the larger order of the Greek divine world.

## Persephone and the Establishment of the Underworld Kingdom

The most famous myth involving Hades concerns his marriage to Persephone, daughter of Zeus and Demeter. The fullest surviving early account appears in the *Homeric Hymn to Demeter*, which describes Persephone gathering flowers when the earth opens beneath her. Hades emerges in his chariot and carries her into the underworld, acting with the prior authorization of Zeus. Persephone resists and cries out for help, but her father has already agreed to the marriage. Demeter eventually discovers what has happened and withdraws her gifts from the earth, causing a famine that threatens the survival of humanity. <sup id="citation-ref-7"><a href="#citation-note-7" aria-label="Read source note 7">[7]</a></sup>

The crisis forces Zeus to intervene. With mortals facing starvation and the gods deprived of the sacrifices offered by their worshippers, Hermes is sent to the underworld to arrange Persephone's return. Hades agrees to release her, but before she departs, he gives her a pomegranate seed to eat. The act ensures that she cannot remain permanently with her mother, and the resulting settlement divides her time between the world above and her husband's kingdom.

The hymn portrays Hades as a ruler operating within an established hierarchy of divine relationships. Zeus's authority has consequences for his marriage, while Demeter possesses sufficient power to force the other gods to reconsider their decisions. Hades eventually complies with the arrangement, although he ensures that Persephone retains a permanent connection to the underworld. His conduct in the story is deliberate and calculating, particularly in the episode involving the pomegranate.

The marriage also establishes Persephone's position as queen of the dead. Before permitting her departure, Hades promises her exceptional honors and authority over the inhabitants of his kingdom. Those who neglect the reverence owed to her will suffer punishment. Their relationship is therefore more than a mythological explanation of agricultural change. It establishes a divine household in which Persephone shares the dignity and authority of underworld sovereignty, a position reflected in later religious worship and artistic representations. <sup id="citation-ref-8"><a href="#citation-note-8" aria-label="Read source note 8">[8]</a></sup>

At the same time, the myth connects Hades directly with Demeter and the productive powers of the earth. The withdrawal of agricultural fertility threatens the living world, while Persephone's eventual return permits its restoration. The underworld becomes part of a wider religious relationship involving the concealment and reappearance of life, a relationship that would grow especially important in the worship of Hades under the name Plouton.

## Herakles and the God Who Could Be Wounded

Although Hades was regarded as an unyielding ruler, one of the earliest surviving stories about him contains a surprising episode of physical vulnerability. In the fifth book of the *Iliad*, the goddess Dione recalls an occasion when Herakles struck Hades with an arrow. The injury caused the god severe pain, forcing him to travel to Olympus, where the divine healer Paeon treated his wound. Hades remained immortal, but he was no more immune to physical suffering than the other gods who endured injuries during their conflicts. <sup id="citation-ref-9"><a href="#citation-note-9" aria-label="Read source note 9">[9]</a></sup> <sup id="citation-ref-10"><a href="#citation-note-10" aria-label="Read source note 10">[10]</a></sup>

Homer provides few details about the circumstances of the confrontation. The encounter is associated with Pylos, but the surviving passage does not explain why Hades became involved or how the struggle began. Later authors and ancient commentators attempted to connect the incident with other traditions concerning Herakles' military expeditions. Modern scholarship has examined these explanations without finding a universally accepted reconstruction of the original story.

The connection with Pylos becomes especially interesting when we return to the temple at Elis. Pausanias records a local tradition that Hades had fought on the side of the Pylians when Herakles attacked their city. According to this explanation, the Eleans honored Hades because he had been an enemy of Herakles, while the god's connection with Pylos provided a reason for his particular relationship with the region. Pausanias explicitly connects the local account with the passage in Homer describing the wound inflicted upon Hades. <sup id="citation-ref-11"><a href="#citation-note-11" aria-label="Read source note 11">[11]</a></sup>

The story demonstrates how local religious communities could preserve interpretations of mythology that differed from the more familiar accounts. Hades was not merely an abstract representation of death to the people who honored him at Elis. He was associated with a remembered divine conflict and understood as a power whose actions had particular significance for their region. The evidence does not tell us when this tradition developed, but its preservation alongside the unusual customs of the sanctuary gives the cult a distinctive historical character.

Herakles encountered Hades again in the story of his final labor, which required him to descend into the underworld and bring Cerberus into the world of the living. In the account attributed to Apollodorus, the hero enters the underworld through a passage at Taenarum and eventually requests permission to take the guardian hound. Hades agrees on the condition that Herakles subdue Cerberus without using his weapons. The hero succeeds, presents the creature to Eurystheus, and subsequently returns it to the underworld. <sup id="citation-ref-12"><a href="#citation-note-12" aria-label="Read source note 12">[12]</a></sup>

This version of the myth portrays Hades exercising the authority of a sovereign who can grant a carefully limited exception. Herakles does not simply seize the hound without consequence. The permission of the underworld ruler is part of the story, and Cerberus eventually returns to his proper place. Even when an extraordinary hero crosses the boundary between life and death, the normal arrangement of the cosmos must ultimately be restored.

The same labor brings Herakles into contact with other inhabitants of the underworld. Apollodorus describes him attempting to rescue Theseus and Pirithous, who had descended with the intention of abducting Persephone. Theseus is released, but the attempt to free Pirithous fails. The episode reinforces the seriousness of violating the underworld's authority and the danger of treating its queen as someone who could be carried away like an ordinary captive.

## Orpheus and the Possibility of Return

The story of Orpheus and Eurydice presents another challenge to the permanence of death. After Eurydice dies, Orpheus descends into the underworld and attempts to recover her through the extraordinary power of his music. In the familiar versions preserved by Virgil and Ovid, his performance moves the rulers of the dead sufficiently that they permit Eurydice to follow him back toward the living world, provided he does not look behind him before completing the journey.

The condition ultimately defeats him. Orpheus turns before they have reached safety, and Eurydice is lost again. The story became one of the most enduring accounts of love and bereavement in classical literature, although its surviving versions differ in important details and the elaborate Roman narratives should not be assumed to reproduce every element of earlier Greek traditions. <sup id="citation-ref-13"><a href="#citation-note-13" aria-label="Read source note 13">[13]</a></sup> <sup id="citation-ref-14"><a href="#citation-note-14" aria-label="Read source note 14">[14]</a></sup>

For Hades, the myth is significant because it presents a ruler capable of responding to an appeal. The release of Eurydice is extraordinary, but the conditions surrounding it preserve the seriousness of the boundary between the living and the dead. Orpheus must succeed under terms established by the underworld powers, and his failure leaves the ordinary separation intact.

The combined stories of Herakles and Orpheus reveal a god whose authority was not simply an expression of hostility toward the living. Hades could permit exceptional departures from his kingdom, but the privilege was never treated as something mortals could demand. Greek storytellers used these encounters to examine the limits of human ability, the consequences of overconfidence, and the grief produced by a separation that even the greatest heroes could rarely overcome.

## Plouton and the Wealth Beneath the Earth

The identity of Hades becomes more complicated when we encounter him under the name Plouton. The name is connected with the Greek word *ploutos*, meaning wealth, and ancient authors recognized the relationship between the underworld ruler and the riches associated with the earth. Plouton eventually became a prominent designation of the god, particularly in religious settings that emphasized fertility and abundance.

One of the most revealing ancient discussions appears in Plato's *Cratylus*. During a conversation about the origins of divine names, Socrates explains that Plouton is associated with wealth because riches emerge from beneath the earth. He also considers why people fear Hades and why they prefer names that avoid directly emphasizing the god's association with the unseen world. His interpretation develops into an unexpectedly favorable description of the underworld ruler, whom he portrays as possessing remarkable wisdom and persuasive power. <sup id="citation-ref-15"><a href="#citation-note-15" aria-label="Read source note 15">[15]</a></sup> <sup id="citation-ref-16"><a href="#citation-note-16" aria-label="Read source note 16">[16]</a></sup>

Socrates suggests that Hades might retain souls through the strength of his speech rather than through physical compulsion. The argument is part of Plato's philosophical exploration of language, and its etymological explanations should not be mistaken for established historical facts. Nevertheless, the passage demonstrates that classical Greek thinkers could associate the god with knowledge, persuasion, and abundance, qualities that contrast sharply with the image of an exclusively terrifying ruler.

The relationship between Hades and Plouton also reflects the importance of agriculture in Greek life. The earth received the buried dead, but it also produced grain, fruit, and the vegetation upon which livestock depended. Its hidden depths contained valuable materials, while its cultivated surface supplied the resources needed to sustain households and cities. The distinction between the world of the living and the kingdom below remained important, yet the two were joined through the earth itself.

In religious art, Plouton could carry a cornucopia, the horn of plenty associated with fertility and prosperity. The attribute emphasized the productive aspect of his divine power, especially in cultic settings involving Demeter and Kore. His connection with agricultural abundance did not require worshippers to forget his authority over death. Both belonged to the same divine identity, although particular communities might emphasize one aspect more strongly than another.

This association makes it necessary to distinguish Plouton from Ploutos, the separate Greek god of wealth. Their names are related, and their artistic symbolism could overlap, but they were not originally the same mythological figure. Hesiod describes Ploutos as the son of Demeter and the mortal Iasion, conceived in a cultivated field in Crete. Ploutos travels throughout the world bestowing riches upon those who encounter him. Plouton, by contrast, designates the underworld ruler whose wealth is connected with the resources and productive capacities of the earth. <sup id="citation-ref-17"><a href="#citation-note-17" aria-label="Read source note 17">[17]</a></sup>

The distinction matters because it reveals different ways in which the Greeks conceived divine abundance. Ploutos personified wealth as something that could come into human possession, while Plouton connected prosperity with a powerful deity whose dominion included the hidden world beneath the cultivated land. Their associations could converge, but their mythological histories were different.

## The Worship of Hades at Elis and Hermione

The sanctuary at Elis is among the most striking pieces of evidence for the historical worship of Hades, but it was not the only place where the underworld ruler received religious honors. Pausanias described the Eleans as the only people he knew who worshipped Hades, a statement that has sometimes been repeated as though it established the absence of his cult elsewhere. The surviving evidence is more complicated, particularly because the god could be honored under names other than Hades.

At Hermione, a city in the Argolid, Pausanias encountered a religious landscape containing several places associated with the underworld. The most prominent sanctuary belonged to Demeter Chthonia, the earth-associated form of Demeter who received an elaborate annual festival. Pausanias describes a procession involving priests, magistrates, and local participants, followed by the sacrifice of cows within the goddess's sanctuary. The rites were sufficiently distinctive that he devoted considerable attention to their procedures and restrictions. <sup id="citation-ref-18"><a href="#citation-note-18" aria-label="Read source note 18">[18]</a></sup>

Opposite this sanctuary stood a temple dedicated to Klymenos, where sacrifices were offered. Pausanias identified Klymenos as a title of the deity who ruled beneath the earth, rather than accepting the local tradition that treated him as an early human figure associated with the sanctuary's foundation. Behind the temple of Demeter Chthonia were three additional sacred places, known as those of Klymenos, Plouton, and the Acherusian Lake. One contained a chasm that local tradition identified as the passage through which Herakles had brought Cerberus back into the world of the living.

The description provides a glimpse into the relationship between mythology, landscape, and cult. The people of Hermione recognized particular locations as connected with underworld powers, and their sanctuaries stood within a wider religious environment dominated by the worship of Demeter Chthonia. The chasm associated with Cerberus gave a familiar myth a place in the local landscape, while the names Klymenos and Plouton preserved different ways of referring to the divine authority beneath the earth.

The evidence from Elis and Hermione also demonstrates why the worship of Hades cannot be reconstructed from his name alone. The title Klymenos, often understood as an honorific or euphemistic designation, could be used in circumstances where worshippers preferred not to emphasize the more frightening name of the god. Other underworld-associated titles and identities complicate the record further. These names were not necessarily interchangeable in every local tradition, and individual cults require examination on their own terms.

Modern scholarship has paid particular attention to this variation. Diana Burton's study of Hades at Elis and Triphylia examines the contrast between the god's formidable association with death and his more beneficial identity as a giver of fertility. Her analysis suggests that these apparently different characteristics should not be treated as unrelated additions to the mythology. They reflect different aspects of the power attributed to a god whose dominion lay beneath the earth. <sup id="citation-ref-19"><a href="#citation-note-19" aria-label="Read source note 19">[19]</a></sup>

The restrictive customs at Elis and the religious landscape of Hermione therefore offer complementary evidence. One emphasizes the inaccessibility of the underworld ruler, while the other places him near a goddess whose worship concerned the fertile earth. Together, they reveal a deity whose cultic significance extended beyond the fearful reputation preserved in epic poetry.

## Offerings and Religious Approaches to Hades

The surviving evidence does not provide a single standardized form of worship practiced by all Greeks who honored Hades. Rituals differed between regions and periods, and descriptions found in mythology cannot always be assumed to reproduce ordinary religious practice. Nevertheless, several ancient texts reveal how offerings to the underworld powers were imagined and how worshippers might seek their attention.

In the tenth book of the *Odyssey*, Circe instructs Odysseus in the preparations required to communicate with the dead. He is to travel to a remote region near the boundary of the underworld, dig a pit, and pour offerings of milk mixed with honey, wine, and water. He must also make promises to the deceased and sacrifice a ram and a black ewe, directing the animals toward the darkness of Erebus. Prayers are then offered to Hades and Persephone as the ruling powers of the realm. <sup id="citation-ref-20"><a href="#citation-note-20" aria-label="Read source note 20">[20]</a></sup>

The ceremony belongs to a literary account of an extraordinary journey, and its details should not be presented as a universal prescription for worshipping Hades. It nevertheless preserves associations between underworld religion, offerings directed into the earth, and the sacrifice of dark-colored animals. Such features also occur in other ancient religious contexts involving deceased spirits or earth-associated divinities, although their meanings and applications varied.

The distinction between Olympian and underworld religion was not always as rigid as modern summaries suggest. Ancient Greek sacrifices could involve different forms of offering, ritual posture, location, and distribution of the sacrificed animal, but these differences did not establish two completely separate religious systems. The same deity might receive different forms of honor according to the place, occasion, and purpose of the ceremony. Hades' religious identity is particularly difficult to understand when every variation is forced into a single formula.

The evidence from historical sanctuaries is consequently more valuable than attempts to reconstruct an imagined universal ritual. At Elis, the restriction on entry formed part of the local cult's distinctive character. At Hermione, sacrifices to Klymenos occurred within a religious landscape closely connected to Demeter Chthonia. These practices reveal relationships between worshippers and the underworld ruler that cannot be reduced to the fear of death alone.

The name Plouton further suggests that favorable divine action was part of his religious significance. Agricultural abundance, the well-being of households, and the productive capacities of the land were concerns that could bring worshippers into contact with earth-associated powers. Although Hades inspired solemnity and dread, the Greeks did not necessarily regard every approach to him as an attempt to escape punishment or avert disaster.

## Hades in Ancient Greek Art

Ancient artists generally represented Hades as a mature, bearded man whose appearance could resemble that of Zeus or Poseidon. Without an identifying inscription, attribute, or mythological context, some surviving figures are difficult to assign confidently to a particular god. Hades' recognizable attributes included a royal scepter and, in his identity as Plouton, the cornucopia. His association with Cerberus and his position beside Persephone could also establish his identity within a larger composition.

The underworld rulers appear prominently on votive plaques from southern Italy, particularly those associated with the sanctuary of Persephone at Locri. Some depict Hades and Persephone enthroned together, receiving offerings or appearing within scenes concerned with marriage and divine sovereignty. Their royal presentation is significant because it shows how the underworld household could be understood through familiar symbols of dignity and authority rather than through frightening or monstrous forms.

Fourth-century BCE South Italian vase painters developed especially elaborate representations of the underworld. One remarkable example is the monumental krater discovered near Altamura, now held by the National Archaeological Museum of Naples. Its painted scenes contain more than twenty mythological figures, including Hades, Persephone, Hermes, Herakles, Orpheus, and Sisyphus. The divine rulers occupy a prominent architectural setting, while other figures illustrate the varied stories associated with the kingdom of the dead. The composition offers an unusually detailed view of how artists imagined the underworld as an inhabited realm possessing its own hierarchy and order. <sup id="citation-ref-21"><a href="#citation-note-21" aria-label="Read source note 21">[21]</a></sup> <sup id="citation-ref-22"><a href="#citation-note-22" aria-label="Read source note 22">[22]</a></sup>

Hades was also associated with a helmet or cap that could render its wearer invisible. In the fifth book of the *Iliad*, Athena uses the helmet of Hades to conceal herself from Ares during battle. The object reflects the god's connection with the unseen, although it does not appear consistently in his surviving artistic representations. His scepter, throne, and association with Persephone were often more useful indications of his identity.

The artistic evidence is important because it challenges the modern tendency to imagine Hades as a monstrous or demonic figure. Ancient Greek artists frequently represented him as a dignified sovereign, distinguished from other gods principally by his attributes and the setting in which he appeared. Even scenes portraying the terrifying inhabitants of the underworld generally reserved a position of royal authority for Hades and Persephone.

## The Ruler Beneath the Living World

Hades occupied a distinctive position in ancient Greek religion because his power extended over a realm that every mortal would eventually encounter, yet few could claim to understand. His kingdom was largely inaccessible to the living, and its inhabitants remained separated from the households and communities they had left behind. The solemn customs surrounding his sanctuary at Elis reflected one aspect of this relationship, while the stories of heroes descending into the underworld expressed the extraordinary difficulty of crossing its boundaries.

The same god could nevertheless be approached as Plouton, whose connection with the hidden riches of the earth associated him with fertility and abundance. His worship at Hermione, his appearance beside Demeter and Persephone, and his representation with the horn of plenty reveal religious dimensions that are easily lost when his identity is reduced to the fear of death.

These different traditions did not produce a single uniform portrait. The Hades of Homer was particularly formidable and difficult to persuade, while the Plouton of later religious and philosophical accounts could embody beneficent powers arising from the earth. Local worshippers emphasized different aspects of his character according to their traditions, and ancient artists represented him through the symbols of kingship as well as abundance.

The relationship between these identities becomes clearer when we consider the importance of the earth itself. The same ground that sustained cultivated fields also received the dead. Crops emerged from beneath its surface, valuable resources were discovered within it, and mortal bodies eventually returned to it. Greek religion did not necessarily regard these processes as contradictory. They belonged to a world in which life depended upon powers that remained largely hidden from human sight.

Hades governed that hidden world. His authority made him one of the most formidable figures in Greek mythology, while his association with Plouton connected him to the resources upon which mortal prosperity depended. The god whose sanctuary could be entered only once a year was also a deity whose power was present in the earth beneath every cultivated field.

For the ancient Greeks, the underworld was therefore more than a distant kingdom awaiting the dead. It belonged to the same earth that sustained the living, and Hades was its enduring sovereign.
